\section{Summary}

WaRF was run with TypeSafe's Jev as its only model, on real defects from the
G+Smo history. A panel of five persona seats, three on \texttt{jev-1.14} and two
on \texttt{jev-1.13}, aggregated in WaRF's log-odds mode after learning from
132 labelled reviews (\emph{LO}), reviewed 220 fresh changes correctly in
\textbf{73.2\%} of cases (balanced accuracy), against \textbf{67.7\%} for one
Jev. This is the first preregistered test in which WaRF beat its single
reviewer (exact McNemar $p = 0.012$, Holm-adjusted $0.024$). Because it was the
sixth comparison on those changes, LO was then frozen and tested once on a
second project, MFEM: on 300 fresh changes it scored \textbf{80.0\%} against
\textbf{75.3\%} for one Jev (23 against 9 changes, $p = 0.020$), with a
calibration learned only on G+Smo. One Claude Opus scores 85.5\% on the G+Smo
fresh set.

% =============================================================================
\section{Data and Measure}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item[Defective changes] C++ changes in the gismo6 history that a later fix
  blamed (B-SZZ), kept only if two independent judges, who see the fix,
  agree that the change introduced the defect. The reviewers never see the fix.
\item[Clean changes] Changes whose added lines all survive unchanged at
  \texttt{origin/dev}, one per defective change, matched by date and size.
\item[Development set] 132 changes (66 + 66), used for all tuning and learning.
\item[Fresh set] 220 changes (110 + 110), never used for tuning; every test on
  it was written down before the first call.
\item[Input] The diff with 30 lines of context and the enclosing function
  bodies, as WaRF sends it.
\item[Measure] Balanced accuracy (BA), the mean of the share of defects caught
  and the share of clean changes passed. Counting added lines alone ranks the
  fresh set with AUC 0.79, so size is a strong confound.
\end{description}

% =============================================================================
\section{Jev}

Jev (TypeSafe System One) is a decision model: it takes a state and typed
questions and returns, per question, a probability for each option, with no
text. WaRF asks one choice question, APPROVE or REJECT, with the seat's persona
as its instructions. Two versions are served through OpenRouter:
\texttt{jev-1.14} (2026-10-14) and \texttt{jev-1.13} (2026-09-17). A call costs
about \$0.0001--0.0002; an LO review (five calls) about \$0.0006.

Jev is stable but forms one opinion per change: identical calls agree on the
decision in 97--99\% of cases, and its scores correlate 0.84--0.97 across
wordings, input formats, retrieved context and the two versions.

\subsection{Aggregation (ADR 0001)}

Each seat $s$ returns $p_s = p(\mathrm{REJECT})$, read to two decimals and
clamped to $[0.01, 0.99]$. Its log-odds for APPROVE is
$r_s = \ln\bigl((1-p_s)/p_s\bigr)$.

\textbf{Calibration.} From the labelled history of that seat (same persona,
model and evidence source), WaRF fits Platt scaling once it has at least 10
labelled reviews of each class:
\[
  e_s = a_s + b_s\, r_s - \pi_s,
\]
where $\pi_s$ is the logit of the smoothed clean share of the training labels,
so $e_s$ is a likelihood ratio rather than a posterior. Without enough history,
$r_s$ is capped at the seat's vote-mode step $\omega_s \cdot \mathrm{LLR}$.

\textbf{Pooling.} Seats of one model shown the same input form one group $g$;
a group contributes the mean of its seats' evidence, and the groups are summed:
\[
  \Lambda = \sum_{g} \frac{1}{|g|} \sum_{s \in g} e_s .
\]
The three \texttt{jev-1.14} seats thus count as one source and the two
\texttt{jev-1.13} seats as another (SPECIFICATION \S3.1: one model is one fault
domain sampled $n$ times).

\textbf{Decision.} After each complete group, WaRF stops if
$\Lambda \ge A \approx 2.890$ (APPROVE) or $\Lambda \le B \approx -2.251$
(REJECT), unless two groups point in opposite directions by at least $\ln 2$
each. If no boundary is crossed, it decides APPROVE if $\Lambda \ge \tau$,
with $\tau = \operatorname{logit}\bigl((1+\theta)/2\bigr)$: 0 for LOGIC,
0.405 for SECURITY, $-0.201$ for PERFORMANCE.

\subsection{Learning}

The panel reviewed the 132 development changes; after each review the known
answer was recorded with \texttt{apply\_oracle} and \texttt{append\_session},
WaRF's own labelling path. The resulting session log is LO's calibration
history. No fresh change entered it.

\section{Results on the Fresh Set}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Reviewer (220 fresh changes) & Right & Caught & False alarms \\
\midrule
One Jev (usual question, threshold from dev)      & 67.7\% & 73/110 & 34/110 \\
WaRF Jev, 7 narrow checks per hunk                & 71.4\% & 67/110 & 20/110 \\
WaRF Jev, narrow checks of both versions          & 71.8\% & 70/110 & 22/110 \\
WaRF panel, untrained, vote mode                  & 69.5\% & 85/110 & 42/110 \\
WaRF panel, learned weights, vote mode            & 69.1\% & 85/110 & 43/110 \\
\textbf{LO: WaRF panel, learned, log-odds mode}   & \textbf{73.2\%} & 83/110 & 32/110 \\
\midrule
One Claude Opus, default effort                   & 85.5\% & 91/110 & 13/110 \\
WaRF Claude, three efforts, reject if any rejects & 86.8\% & 100/110 & 19/110 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{LO against one Jev:} only LO right on 16 changes, only one Jev on 4;
$p = 0.012$, Holm $0.024$ over the two arms of that test. The preregistered
verdict is a win. LO against the untrained panel: $+3.6$ points ($p = 0.077$);
against the narrow checks: $+1.8$ points ($p = 0.61$).

The seats' answers are the same in the vote-mode and log-odds arms; the gain
comes from the aggregation. Calibrated evidence pooled per model keeps most of
the extra defects the panel catches while cutting its false alarms from 42 to 32.

\section{Second Repository: MFEM}

Because the G+Smo fresh set had been used for six comparisons, LO was frozen
and tested once on a second C++ project, MFEM (github.com/mfem/mfem, read with
git only). The test was preregistered before any change was built.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item[Set] B-SZZ candidates from 2,949 fix commits on \texttt{master}; judge A
  judged 360 candidates (157 yes), judge B judged those 157 (151 yes); the first
  150 confirmed defects were kept, with 150 surviving clean changes matched by
  date and size.
\item[LO] Exactly as tested on G+Smo, calibrated only on the 132 G+Smo
  development reviews; nothing from MFEM entered it.
\item[One Jev] The usual question, REJECT at $p \ge 0.79$ (fixed on G+Smo).
\end{description}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Reviewer (300 MFEM changes) & Right & Caught & False alarms \\
\midrule
One Jev                                   & 75.3\% & 94/150 & 18/150 \\
WaRF Jev, 7 narrow checks per hunk        & 74.3\% & 88/150 & 15/150 \\
\textbf{LO: WaRF log-odds panel}          & \textbf{80.0\%} & 112/150 & 22/150 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{LO against one Jev:} only LO right on 23 changes, only one Jev on 9;
exact McNemar $p = 0.020$. The preregistered verdict is a win. LO also beats
the narrow checks (29 against 12, $p = 0.012$); the narrow checks do not beat
one Jev here. On the 87 defects visible from the diff LO scores 85.2\% against
80.8\%. Ranked by the seats' mean $p$, LO's AUC is 0.883 against 0.866 for the
count of added lines: the gain lies in WaRF's decision, not in a better ranking.

\section{Development of LO}

LO's decision depends only on the seats' stored $p_s$ and the labelled history,
so it was rebuilt offline with WaRF's own calibrator, grouping and decision
functions. The rebuild reproduces all 220 logged decisions. Variants were judged
by stratified 10-fold cross-validation, repeated five times, over all 352 judged
changes, calibrating on the training folds only; no further look at the fresh
set was spent. Repeats vary by about $\pm 0.4$ points.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Cross-validated, 352 changes & Right \\
\midrule
One Jev seat in WaRF (usual question, calibrated)  & 73.8\% \\
\textbf{LO, five personas}                         & \textbf{76.3\%} \\
LO + the usual question on both versions           & 76.7\% \\
LO + the 7 narrow checks of both versions          & 76.4\% \\
LO + usual question + narrow checks                & 75.5\% \\
LO + a context seat (retrieved declarations)       & 75.2\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

More Jev seats do not move LO: they repeat what the personas already say. A
context seat, shown the declarations of the names on the changed lines, helps a
little on its own (74.1\%) but lowers LO: as a separate source its evidence is
summed with the two Jev groups although it mostly repeats them.

\subsection{Cold Start}

A new user has no labelled history, so every seat starts uncalibrated:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Labelled reviews before the fresh set & LO right \\
\midrule
none                         & 68.6\% \\
10 defective + 10 clean      & 70.5\% \\
15 + 15                      & 71.4\% \\
20 + 20                      & 73.6\% \\
66 + 66 (development set)    & 73.2\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Without history LO is about one Jev. It needs 10--20 confirmed defects, which in
a real project may take months to collect; clean labels are easy.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Both projects are C++ numerics libraries (isogeometric analysis and
  finite elements); other kinds of code are untested.
\item Jev's ranking stays close to a count of added lines (G+Smo fresh set
  0.75--0.77 against 0.79; MFEM 0.88 against 0.87); much of any Jev score is
  change size.
\item LO asks five seats where one Jev asks one: about \$0.00065 against
  \$0.00013 per review.
\item The labels rest on two Claude judges who see the later fix.
\item The clean controls are clean only in that their lines survived; a few may
  hold unfound defects.
\item Claude Opus remains about 12 points ahead of LO.
\end{itemize}
